# CRM Implementation Checklist: Track Leads From Form Submission to Revenue

A CRM implementation should do more than store contacts.

For marketing and sales teams, the real goal is to track the path from form submission to qualified lead, opportunity, and revenue.

Use this checklist before launching or replacing a CRM.

## 1. Define the lead journey

Map the basic path:

1. Visitor lands on page
2. Visitor submits form
3. Contact is created
4. Lead source is preserved
5. Sales follows up
6. Lead becomes qualified
7. Opportunity is created
8. Revenue is won or lost

If the journey is unclear, reporting will be unclear too.

## 2. Decide which forms matter

List the forms that create meaningful sales or marketing signals.

Examples:

- Contact form
- Demo request
- Consultation request
- Pricing request
- Download form
- Newsletter form
- Webinar form

Not every form needs the same reporting weight. A demo request usually matters more than a newsletter signup.

## 3. Capture source data

Before launch, decide which fields need to be captured.

Useful fields include:

- Original source
- Source / medium
- Campaign
- Landing page
- Form name or form ID
- Conversion page
- UTM parameters
- Referrer
- Conversion date

This data is much easier to preserve at the moment of form submission than to recreate later.

## 4. Use hidden fields carefully

Hidden fields can capture useful tracking values without asking the visitor to type them manually.

Common examples:

- utm_source
- utm_medium
- utm_campaign
- landing_page
- form_id
- conversion_page

Avoid collecting unnecessary personal information. Track what helps the business understand lead quality and source performance.

## 5. Connect the CRM to analytics

The CRM does not need to replace GA4 or Google Tag Manager.

A strong setup lets each tool do its job:

- GTM tracks website behavior
- GA4 reports website events and funnel behavior
- CRM stores contacts, lifecycle stages, pipeline, and revenue outcomes

For HubSpot forms, see the guide on [tracking HubSpot forms with Google Tag Manager](/articles/hubspot-form-tracking-google-tag-manager/).

## 6. Define lifecycle stages

Lifecycle stages help the team separate raw leads from better opportunities.

A simple model might include:

- Lead
- Marketing qualified lead
- Sales qualified lead
- Opportunity
- Customer

The exact names matter less than consistent usage.

## 7. Assign ownership and follow-up

A CRM implementation fails if leads enter the system but no one owns follow-up.

Define:

- Who receives each form submission
- How fast follow-up should happen
- When a lead becomes qualified
- When a deal should be created
- What happens if sales cannot reach the lead

The CRM should support the process, not replace it.

## 8. Build reports before launch

Decide which reports matter before the CRM goes live.

Useful starter reports include:

- Leads by source
- Leads by form
- Qualified leads by source
- Opportunities by campaign
- Revenue by source
- Form submission to opportunity rate
- Lead response time

If leadership will not use a report, do not overbuild it.

## 9. Test before publishing

Before launch, submit test leads from important forms.

Confirm:

- Contact records are created
- Source data is preserved
- Hidden fields work
- Sales receives the lead
- Lifecycle stage rules work
- GA4 events fire
- CRM reports show the test data

Testing one form is not enough. Test the important conversion paths.

## 10. Keep the first version simple

The first CRM implementation should be useful, not perfect.

Start with the fields, forms, lifecycle stages, and reports that support real decisions. Add complexity only when the team needs it.

## Bottom line

A good CRM implementation connects form submissions to source data, follow-up, pipeline, and revenue.

The goal is not just to collect more leads. The goal is to understand which leads are worth pursuing and which marketing activities create real sales opportunities.
